Absolutely. Isaiah 3 has several strong themes for group discussion: leadership, responsibility, pride, social injustice, judgment, and the consequences of a society turning away from God.

Here are **7 discussion questions** designed to provoke conversation rather than produce a simple “correct” answer:

1. **Isaiah 3:1–3 — What happens to a society when dependable leadership and stability are removed?**  
   What kinds of people or institutions do we depend on for stability, perhaps more than we realize?

2. **Isaiah 3:4–7 — Why do you think Isaiah describes inexperienced or unwilling leadership as part of Judah’s judgment?**  
   What qualities does Scripture suggest we should look for in a good leader?

3. **Isaiah 3:8–9 — The people openly display their sin rather than hiding it. Why might a society eventually stop being ashamed of behavior it once recognized as wrong?**  
   Is there a difference between acknowledging our sin honestly and becoming proud of it?

4. **Isaiah 3:10–11 — Why does God interrupt this chapter of judgment to say that things will ultimately go well for the righteous?**  
   What encouragement might these verses offer someone trying to live faithfully in a society moving in another direction?

5. **Isaiah 3:13–15 — God specifically condemns leaders for “crushing” His people and exploiting the poor. Why does God hold leaders especially accountable for how vulnerable people are treated?**  
   What responsibilities come with authority?

6. **Isaiah 3:16–24 — Why does Isaiah spend so much time describing clothing, jewelry, appearance, and luxury?**  
   Is the problem the possessions themselves, or what those possessions reveal about pride, priorities, and the condition of the heart?

7. **Looking at Isaiah 3 as a whole — Is this chapter mainly about individual sin, corrupt leadership, a broken society, or all three working together?**  
   Where do you see the connection between **personal choices and the health of an entire community**?

I especially like **#7 as the closing question** because there isn't an easy one-sentence answer. It can bring the entire chapter together and give everyone in the group something to contribute.


Yes. Isaiah 4 is short—only six verses—but it gives your group a very different direction from chapter 3. After judgment, Isaiah turns toward **restoration, cleansing, holiness, God’s presence, and protection**.

Here are **7 conversation-provoking questions for Isaiah 4**:

1. **Isaiah 4:1 — How does this verse connect with the judgment described in Isaiah 3?**  
   What does it tell us about how the consequences of a society’s choices can affect people who were not necessarily responsible for making those choices?

2. **Isaiah 4:2 — Isaiah suddenly introduces “the Branch of the LORD.” What do you think this image of a branch communicates after all the destruction of chapter 3?**  
   Why might new growth be such a powerful picture of hope?

3. **Isaiah 4:3 — What does it mean to be called “holy”?**  
   Is holiness primarily about our behavior, our relationship with God, our identity as God’s people—or some combination of these?

4. **Isaiah 4:4 — Isaiah describes God as washing away the filth of Jerusalem and cleansing its bloodstains. Why do you think restoration begins with cleansing rather than simply rebuilding what was lost?**  
   What might that teach us about repentance and restoration in our own lives?

5. **Isaiah 4:5 — The cloud by day and fire by night strongly recall God leading Israel through the wilderness. Why would Isaiah use that imagery here?**  
   What might Isaiah's audience have understood when they heard those words?

6. **Isaiah 4:5–6 — God promises both His presence and protection. What is the difference between asking God to remove difficult circumstances and trusting God to be present with us through them?**

7. **Looking at Isaiah 3 and 4 together — Chapter 3 is dominated by judgment, while chapter 4 ends with cleansing, protection, and hope. What does this combination teach us about the character of God?**  
   **Can God's judgment and God's mercy actually be parts of the same work of restoration?**

For a group discussion, **#7 could be especially interesting** because it connects the two chapters you have now studied. It moves the conversation beyond “God judges” versus “God is merciful” and asks whether Isaiah may be presenting **judgment, cleansing, and restoration as connected rather than contradictory**.
